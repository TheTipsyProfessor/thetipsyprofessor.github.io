Rahul has produced interviews for radio for eleven years, which means he has strong views about a format I have only ever been on the wrong end of. We watched an episode of Kunal Kamra's show on a phone, on a platform in the Sacred Valley, with an hour to wait and a beer each — a comedian sitting down with the historian Ramachandra Guha — and then argued about why that pairing works at all.^[I am not going to summarise the episode. It is embedded above and it makes its own case better than I would. What follows is about the *form*.]

::: exchange
TP: Start with the objection, because I had it too. A comedian interviewing a historian sounds like a category error.

RM: It sounds like one if you think the interviewer's job is to know things. It isn't. The interviewer's job is to be *curious in public* on behalf of someone who isn't there.

TP: And a journalist can't do that?

RM: A journalist often can't afford to. They've got a line to pursue, a running order, and usually eleven minutes. Eleven minutes makes you extract. You can't be curious in eleven minutes — you can only collect.

TP: That's the thing I couldn't name. It's not that the long format has more room. It's that the short one forces a *posture*.

RM: Right. And the posture leaks into the questions. Short interviews are full of questions that already contain their answer. "Wouldn't you say that—" is not a question, it's a request for a signature.
:::

::: aside

::: exchange
TP: What does the comedian actually bring, then? Beyond having the time.

RM: Permission to be stupid. He can ask the question everybody has and nobody senior will risk — the one that sounds naïve. A historian gets asked sophisticated questions all day. Almost nobody asks him the obvious one and then *waits*.

TP: The waiting again.

RM: Always the waiting. Watch where the good material is in any long interview. It's never in the answer to the question. It's in the second thing they say, after a silence, when they've decided the first answer was insufficient.

TP: So the skill is not asking. It's not filling.

RM: The skill is being comfortable while somebody else is uncomfortable. Which, incidentally, is also stand-up.
:::

## What I'm taking from it

Not a technique. A constraint: don't ask anything I already have a view about.

That is much harder than it sounds and I failed it twice in the conversation above — both times, embarrassingly, while agreeing with him.^[The second one is at roughly the eleven-minute mark, where I supply his argument for him and he has the grace not to say so.]

> The interview isn't the questions. It's the silence you're willing to leave after them.

We watched the rest without talking until the train came, which Rahul pointed out was the first correct thing I'd done all afternoon.
