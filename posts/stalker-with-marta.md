Marta programmes a cinema and has seen this film nineteen times. I have now seen it twice, eleven years apart, and disliked it differently on each occasion.

We recorded the argument rather than writing it up, because neither of us was going to concede in prose.^[I did concede, slightly, at minute fifty-one. It is audible.]

::: exchange
MO: You said last time that nothing happens. I want to start there, because it's wrong in an interesting way.

TP: I said nothing happens *for very long stretches*, which is different and also true.

MO: It's true and it's the point. The film is about the cost of getting somewhere. If the journey were exciting it would cost nothing.

TP: See, this is the move I can never accept. "It's boring on purpose" is unfalsifiable. Any dull film can claim it.

MO: It's not unfalsifiable. You can ask whether the boredom is *doing* anything. Watch what happens to your attention in the trolley sequence — the four minutes on the rails with no dialogue. By the end of it you're reading the actors' faces harder than you were at the start. It trains you.

TP: Or it exhausts me and I mistake exhaustion for depth.

MO: Those are hard to tell apart from the inside. But there's a test: does it work on a rewatch, when you already know nothing is coming? If it's just withholding, the second viewing collapses. This one doesn't.

TP: Nineteen times, though.

MO: Nineteen times.
:::

::: aside
He has the better rhetorical position here and the worse argument, which is his most familiar condition.
:::

::: exchange
TP: Let me try the honest version rather than the clever one. I think I resent films that require a particular mood to land, because I can't summon that mood on demand and I feel locked out.

MO: That's much better than the boring-on-purpose objection.

TP: It's also less flattering to me.

MO: Usually the way. And I'd say — yes, it requires something. So does a long walk. You wouldn't say a mountain is badly designed because you have to be in the mood for it.
:::

::: exchange
TP: So where does that leave the recommendation? Somebody reads this and has a free evening.

MO: Watch it on a night when you have nowhere to be afterwards. Not tired. Not with someone who checks their phone. If those three things aren't true, wait — it'll keep.

TP: And my version: watch the first forty minutes. If your attention has changed shape by then, stay. If you're just counting, you have my permission to leave, and Marta's contempt.

MO: Not contempt. Disappointment.
:::

No score. The site has an essay three cards away arguing against exactly that, and I am not going to lose an argument to myself in public twice in one month.
