Devika builds ranking models for a living — cricketers, mostly, which in this country is a job with consequences. We were in Dharamshala for entirely unrelated reasons and ended up watching an episode of Kunal Kamra's *Nope*, in which he puts "who was the best prime minister" to the historian Aditya Mukherjee.^[As before, I am not going to summarise it. It's embedded above. What interested us was the *shape* of the question, not the answer anybody gave to it.]

I expected her to enjoy it. She spent the first ten minutes with her arms folded.

::: exchange
TP: You rank people professionally. I assumed "who was the best" was your native language.

DR: It's my job. That's not the same as thinking it's a good question.

TP: Go on.

DR: Any ranking has three things in it. The data, the weights, and the thing you refuse to measure because you can't. People argue about the first, occasionally about the second, and never about the third. The third is where all the actual disagreement lives.

TP: Example.

DR: Batting averages don't contain the fact that a man walked out at forty for three on a wearing pitch in an away Test his team was going to lose. You can build a model that gestures at it. You cannot build one that *contains* it. So every ranking is a ranking of the measurable, wearing the clothes of a ranking of the thing.
:::

::: aside
He tried, at this point, to relate it to the second-cup theorem. She asked him to wait until she had finished.
:::

::: exchange
TP: So is the question useless?

DR: No, and this is the part I've changed my mind on. The question is useless as a question and extremely useful as a *provocation*. Nobody watching that episode cares about the ordering. They care about which criteria got smuggled in — and you only find out what somebody's criteria are by making them rank things.

TP: The answer is worthless but the working is the point.

DR: The working is always the point. Which is why a list is the worst possible way to publish it, and an argument is the best.

TP: You could put that on my masthead.

DR: I'd want a fee.
:::

## What I took from it

That I've been right for the wrong reason.

I've argued in [three separate essays](/essay/against-the-highlight-reel/) that scoring things is a con — and my objection was aesthetic. Scores felt cheap. Devika's objection is structural and much harder to wriggle out of: a score is a *lossy compression* of a judgement, and the part it drops is precisely the part worth publishing.

> Ask the question to find out what someone values. Then throw the answer away and keep the criteria.

We missed the last cable car arguing about whether a bowling average is a better number than a batting average. She thinks it obviously is. I have no idea, and I have three pages of notes.
