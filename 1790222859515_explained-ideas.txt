EXPLAINED: EIGHT ESSAY IDEAS
============================

Working notes for the Explained section. Each idea has a title, a dek, an
opening scene, a story arc in acts, the aspects worth covering, possible
Professor Interrupts asides, a closing image, a plate idea, rough length, and
a list of claims to verify before publishing.

Dates and figures below are from memory, to be checked. Anything marked
[VERIFY] is one I am less sure of, or one that people often get wrong.

House rules, as with the other essays:
- No invented quotes or scenes. Typical characters are fine if they are
  presented as typical.
- Contested science is presented as contested.
- One clear question per essay, answered by the end.

1. WHY INDIA IS HALF AN HOUR OFF
------------------------------------------------------------------------------

Dek: Almost every country on earth sets its clocks a whole number of hours
from Greenwich. India sets them five and a half. The story of that half hour
runs through observatories, railways, a world war, tea gardens and a country
where the sun rises two hours earlier in one corner than the other, and all
of it shares one clock.

The question: Why UTC+5:30, and why only one time zone for a country nearly
3,000 km wide?

Opening scene:
A summer morning in Arunachal Pradesh, where the sun is up around 4:30 or
earlier and offices do not open until 10. Then cut to the same moment in
Kutch, Gujarat, where it is still dark. One country, one clock, and a
two-hour gap in daylight. End the opening on the question: who decided this,
and why the half?

Story arc:

Act I: Before clocks agreed.
- Every town once kept its own time by the sun. Local noon was when the sun
  was highest, and noon in Calcutta came well before noon in Bombay.
- This did not matter until things moved fast enough for minutes to matter:
  first the telegraph, then the railway.
- The Madras Observatory, and "Madras Time", used by the railways across
  much of India in the later nineteenth century. [VERIFY exact dates and extent]

Act II: The railway problem, worldwide.
- The United States in 1883: the railways, not the government, imposed time
  zones on the "day of two noons".
- The International Meridian Conference in Washington, 1884, which made
  Greenwich the prime meridian. France held out for years, and for a while
  used Paris time "retarded by nine minutes and twenty-one seconds" so as not
  to say "Greenwich". [VERIFY wording and year, 1911]
- Switzerland's own story: Bern time, then Central European Time from 1894.
  [VERIFY]

Act III: India picks a meridian.
- Around 1905–06 the British government proposed a single Indian Standard
  Time at 82.5 degrees east, exactly five and a half hours ahead of
  Greenwich, because 82.5 is a multiple of 7.5 (the half-hour meridians).
- Why 82.5: it runs roughly through the middle of the country, near
  Mirzapur/Allahabad (Prayagraj). The reference point later associated with
  it, and where official time is now kept (CSIR–National Physical
  Laboratory, Delhi). [VERIFY the "Shankargarh" reference-point claim]
- Resistance: Calcutta kept Calcutta Time until 1948, and Bombay kept Bombay
  Time until 1955. A city insisting on its own clock as an act of pride.
  [VERIFY both dates]
- The Second World War: clocks moved forward an hour as "war time", 1942–45.
  [VERIFY]

Act IV: The half hour elsewhere.
- Nepal is UTC+5:45, deliberately not India's time. The politics of a
  quarter hour.
- China: one time zone across roughly 5,000 km, with Xinjiang unofficially
  keeping its own time. A useful comparison: a single clock as a statement
  of national unity.
- Newfoundland, Iran, Afghanistan and parts of Australia also use half hours.
  India is not alone, just the biggest.

Act V: The tea garden clock.
- "Chai bagan time": Assam's tea estates, since the British period, kept
  their clocks an hour ahead of IST, to use the early daylight.
- The modern argument for a second time zone for the northeast. The loss of
  productive daylight, electricity costs, schoolchildren going home in the
  dark. Calls from Assam and Arunachal politicians.
- The counter-arguments: railway and flight scheduling, confusion at the
  border between zones, national integration.
- A CSIR–NPL paper around 2018 proposing two zones, IST-I and IST-II.
  [VERIFY date and details]

Act VI: What a clock really is.
- Time zones are not a fact of nature. They are an agreement, and a
  political one.
- The atomic clocks at NPL, and the fact that "Indian Standard Time" is now
  set by caesium atoms, not the sun.
- The Swiss railway clock (Hans Hilfiker, 1944), whose red second hand
  pauses at twelve so every station clock can be reset to the minute.
  Apple's licence for it (2012). The most Swiss object ever made, and a
  perfect symbol of time as something a railway imposes. [VERIFY figures]

Aspects to cover:
- Solar time against clock time: the equation of time, briefly.
- Why 7.5-degree meridians give half-hour offsets.
- Railways as the real inventors of standard time.
- Unity against practicality.
- Biology: body clocks, and why a clock out of step with the sun costs sleep
  and health. Studies on "social jetlag" in India. [VERIFY any specific study]
- Daylight saving: why India does not use it. (It did during the war.)

Possible asides:
- "The professor notes that India has one time zone and twenty-two languages
  listed in the Constitution. We agreed on the clock first."
- On Calcutta holding out until 1948: the empire could impose a railway, but
  it could not make a city give up its noon.

Closing image:
Back in Arunachal at 4:30 a.m., the sun already up, a clock on the wall
insisting it is still night. A clock is a promise a country makes to itself,
and sometimes the sun disagrees.

Plate idea: a railway station clock over a platform at dawn, with a long
shadow pointing the wrong way. Or two clocks on one wall showing different
times: Calcutta and Bombay.

Length: 4 to 5 pours.

Claims to verify:
- Madras Time's use and dates.
- The IST adoption year and the 82.5°E reference point.
- Calcutta Time ending in 1948 and Bombay Time in 1955.
- Wartime clock changes.
- France's Paris time "retarded by nine minutes and twenty-one seconds"
  (1911).
- Swiss adoption of Central European Time in 1894.
- Tea garden time history.
- The NPL two-zone proposal.
- The Swiss railway clock and Apple licence details.

Starting sources: CSIR–NPL on IST; Derek Howse, "Greenwich Time and the
Discovery of the Longitude"; reporting on the northeast time-zone debate; the
SBB and Hilfiker clock history.

2. TWENTY-SEVEN KILOMETRES UNDER THE BORDER
------------------------------------------------------------------------------

Dek: Just outside Geneva, a hundred metres under fields where cows graze on
both sides of the French–Swiss border, is the largest machine ever built.
It exists to answer questions that have no practical use. Along the way it
gave the world the web.

The question: What is CERN actually doing, and why does a machine built for
useless questions keep producing useful things?

Opening scene:
Standing in a field near Meyrin or across the border in France, where
nothing is visible: a road, a farm, a bus stop. Underneath, protons are
going round a 27 km ring about 11,000 times a second, close to the speed of
light. [VERIFY lap rate] The most powerful machine on earth is invisible
from above.

Story arc:

Act I: A laboratory to rebuild Europe.
- 1954: CERN founded by twelve European states, less than a decade after a
  war in which physics had built the atomic bomb. Science as reconciliation;
  a place where former enemies would work on the same problems.
  [VERIFY founding members]
- Why Geneva: neutrality, an international city, and the Swiss hosting
  tradition.
- The convention's rule that CERN does no military work and publishes its
  results openly. [VERIFY wording]

Act II: What a collider is.
- The basic idea, explained for a curious reader: to see what something is
  made of, smash it and look at the pieces. The harder you smash, the
  smaller the pieces you can see.
- E = mc²: collisions turn energy into new particles that did not exist
  before.
- The Large Hadron Collider's parts:
  - the ring, 27 km;
  - superconducting magnets cooled to about 1.9 K, colder than outer space;
  - detectors (ATLAS, CMS) the size of cathedrals, taking pictures of
    collisions many millions of times a second.
  [VERIFY numbers]

Act III: The missing piece.
- The Standard Model: the periodic table of particles, and the question it
  could not answer, which is why anything has mass.
- Peter Higgs and others in 1964 proposed a field that fills the universe,
  and a particle that would prove it.
- The hunt, which took nearly fifty years.
- 10 September 2008: first beam. Then, nine days later, a faulty electrical
  connection causes a helium leak, damages magnets and delays everything by
  over a year. [VERIFY]
- The fear stories: lawsuits claiming the LHC would make a black hole and
  swallow the Earth. Why physicists were not worried: cosmic rays hitting
  the Earth have done the same collisions at higher energies for billions
  of years.
- 4 July 2012: the Higgs discovery announced. Peter Higgs in the audience,
  wiping his eyes. [VERIFY; it is widely reported]
- 2013 Nobel Prize to Higgs and François Englert.

Act IV: The accident that changed everything.
- March 1989: Tim Berners-Lee, a British scientist at CERN, writes a memo
  called "Information Management: A Proposal", to help physicists share
  documents across different computers.
- His boss, Mike Sendall, writes on the cover: "Vague but exciting…"
- The first website, info.cern.ch, 1991.
- 30 April 1993: CERN puts the web software in the public domain. It could
  have been patented. It was not. That decision is arguably CERN's biggest
  gift to the world. [VERIFY dates]

Act V: The useless and the useful.
- Other spin-offs:
  - medical imaging (PET);
  - particle therapy for cancer;
  - the Grid, which led to distributed computing;
  - detector technology.
  [VERIFY each; do not overclaim]
- The argument for curiosity-driven science: Faraday's apocryphal "what use
  is a newborn baby?" [flag as apocryphal] and Robert Wilson's 1969
  testimony to the US Congress, that the Fermilab accelerator had nothing to
  do with defending the country "except to make it worth defending".
  [VERIFY quote]

Act VI: India at CERN.
- India's long involvement, and its associate membership (2017). [VERIFY]
- The statue of Shiva as Nataraja, the cosmic dancer, given by India and
  standing at CERN since 2004. Fritjof Capra's "cosmic dance" of subatomic
  particles. A physics lab with a Hindu god at the door, and the conspiracy
  theories that followed. [VERIFY year]

Aspects to cover:
- What particles are, and what "discovering" one means (statistics,
  five-sigma).
- Antimatter at CERN: the real version of Angels & Demons, and why
  antimatter bombs are fiction.
- Funding: how much it costs, who pays, and how that compares to things
  people do not question. [VERIFY budget figures]
- Big science as a model of international cooperation, including Israeli
  and Palestinian, and Indian and Pakistani, scientists in the same
  collaborations. [VERIFY Pakistan's associate membership]
- What is next: the proposed Future Circular Collider (about 91 km) and the
  debate over whether it is worth it. [VERIFY]
- Open data and open access.

Possible asides:
- On the black hole fear: "The professor notes that the universe has been
  running this experiment for free, at higher energies, for a few billion
  years, and we are still here to worry about it."
- On "the God particle": a name physicists hate, from a book title
  (Lederman's publisher reportedly vetoed "The Goddamn Particle").
  [VERIFY anecdote]

Closing image:
The same quiet field, the bus stop. Somewhere under it, physicists are
still asking why anything weighs anything. And every time you load a web
page, you are using the by-product of that question.

Plate idea: a Swiss–French field at dusk with the dotted line of the buried
ring drawn over it like a map. Or the Nataraja statue against the CERN
buildings.

Length: 5 pours.

Claims to verify:
- Founding year and members; the convention wording.
- LHC specifications.
- Dates of the 2008 incident.
- The Higgs announcement details.
- Berners-Lee's memo and "vague but exciting".
- The 1993 public-domain date.
- India's and Pakistan's associate memberships.
- The Nataraja year.
- The Robert Wilson quote.
- Spin-off claims.
- FCC size.

Starting sources: CERN's own history pages; Berners-Lee, "Weaving the
Web"; the CERN Courier; the 2013 Nobel press release.

3. ONE WIND
------------------------------------------------------------------------------

Dek: Every June, a single seasonal wind decides whether a billion people eat
well, whether governments survive, and whether the stock market rises. The
monsoon is the most important weather system on earth, and one of the
hardest to predict. The attempt to predict it accidentally discovered El
Niño.

The question: What is the monsoon, why is it so hard to forecast, and why
does a country of 1.4 billion still watch the sky every June?

Opening scene:
Early June in Kerala. The first heavy rain on the tin roofs, the smell of
wet earth (petrichor: there is a word, and a chemistry, for it), and the
India Meteorological Department announcing "onset over Kerala" as if it were
a national event. It is. Newspapers track the monsoon's advance up the
country on a map, like a campaign.

Story arc:

Act I: A word and a trade route.
- "Monsoon" comes from the Arabic mawsim, season.
- Ancient sailors learned to ride the seasonal winds across the Arabian Sea.
  The Greek sailor Hippalus is credited with it in the first century, and
  the Periplus of the Erythraean Sea describes the trade. Roman gold in
  south India, pepper in Rome. The monsoon built an economy long before
  anyone understood it. [VERIFY Hippalus attribution, which is debated]

Act II: How it works.
- The simple version: land heats faster than sea. In summer the subcontinent
  becomes a huge low-pressure zone, and moist air from the Indian Ocean
  rushes in.
- The fuller version:
  - the Intertropical Convergence Zone moving north;
  - the Tibetan Plateau as a raised heat source;
  - the Somali jet;
  - the Western Ghats and the Himalaya wringing water out of the air.
- Why Cherrapunji and Mawsynram are among the wettest places on earth, and
  why the Thar is a desert. [VERIFY rainfall records]

Act III: Famine and the birth of forecasting.
- The great famines of the 1870s: tens of millions dead, partly from a
  failed monsoon, and partly from colonial policy (grain exports, relief
  refused). Hedge carefully, and cite Mike Davis's "Late Victorian
  Holocausts".
- 1875: the India Meteorological Department founded. [VERIFY]
- Henry Blanford's first long-range forecasts in the 1880s, based on
  Himalayan snow cover: more snow, weaker monsoon. [VERIFY]

Act IV: The man who found El Niño by looking for rain.
- Sir Gilbert Walker, a Cambridge mathematician, became head of the IMD in
  1904 and set out to predict the monsoon by correlating everything with
  everything, by hand, with teams of human "computers".
- He found a giant see-saw of air pressure between the Pacific and Indian
  oceans: the Southern Oscillation.
- He was mocked. Decades later, in the 1960s, Jacob Bjerknes linked it to
  El Niño, and it became ENSO, the most important climate pattern on the
  planet.
- The monsoon forecast failed, but produced a much bigger discovery.
  [VERIFY dates]

Act V: The stakes today.
- The monsoon brings about 70 to 75 per cent of India's annual rainfall.
  [VERIFY]
- A large share of farmland is rainfed. [VERIFY current figure]
- Drought years and their politics.
- The monsoon in the economy: food inflation, rural demand, even car and
  motorcycle sales.
- Why a "normal" monsoon forecast in April moves markets.

Act VI: A changing monsoon.
- What climate change is doing:
  - fewer rainy days;
  - more extreme downpours;
  - more variable onset;
  - rising floods alongside dry spells;
  - Mumbai's 2005 flood;
  - the Kerala floods of 2018.
  [VERIFY trends; cite IPCC AR6 and IMD]
- Modern forecasting: dynamical models, supercomputers, and why the
  monsoon is still harder to forecast than European weather.

Aspects to cover:
- The monsoon in culture:
  - Kalidasa's Meghaduta (a cloud sent as a messenger);
  - raga Megh Malhar;
  - Bollywood's rain songs.
  Keep this short.
- Petrichor: the chemistry of the smell of rain (geosmin, and the 1964
  paper that named it). [VERIFY]
- The retreating (northeast) monsoon, and Tamil Nadu's different rainy
  season.
- Groundwater: how India pumps more of it than any other country. [VERIFY]

Possible asides:
- On Walker: "The professor notes that the most useful discovery in
  climate science was made by a man who failed at the job he was hired
  for."
- On forecasting failure as a scientific method.

Closing image:
A farmer in Vidarbha or Marathwada looking at the sky in late June, a
satellite model in Pune running the same question at a billion calculations
a second. Both asking the same thing, and neither yet sure.

Plate idea: a wall of dark monsoon cloud over green fields with a single
figure under an umbrella. Or a map of India with the onset isochrones drawn
like a front line.

Length: 5 pours.

Claims to verify:
- Etymology and Hippalus.
- Rainfall records.
- Famine death estimates (give ranges).
- IMD founding.
- Blanford's forecasts.
- Walker's dates and role.
- Bjerknes.
- The share of rainfall and of rainfed agriculture.
- Climate trends.
- Petrichor details.
- The groundwater ranking.

Starting sources: IMD history; Sulochana Gadgil's papers on monsoon
forecasting; Sunil Amrith, "Unruly Waters"; Mike Davis, "Late Victorian
Holocausts"; IPCC AR6, South Asia chapter.

4. WHY THE SECOND COFFEE DOESN'T WORK
------------------------------------------------------------------------------

Dek: Caffeine does not give you energy. It hides the fact that you are
tired. That is why the first cup feels like a miracle and the third feels
like nothing. It is also a story that runs from an Ethiopian legend to a
Sufi saint who smuggled seven beans into India.

The question: What does caffeine actually do in the brain, and why does it
stop working?

Opening scene:
The first cup of the morning, and the ten minutes after it when the world
sharpens. Then the third cup at 3 p.m., which does nothing except make your
hands shake. Same molecule, different result. Why?

Story arc:

Act I: The molecule.
- 1819: the German chemist Friedlieb Runge isolates caffeine. The story
  goes that Goethe gave him the coffee beans and suggested it. [VERIFY]
- What caffeine is: a bitter alkaloid that plants evolved as a pesticide.
  It paralyses and kills insects that eat the plant, and it may also make
  pollinators remember the flower (the 2013 bee study). [VERIFY]
- The joke writes itself: humans took an insecticide and built a morning
  ritual around it.

Act II: How tiredness works.
- Adenosine: a by-product of the brain's energy use that builds up through
  the day and binds to receptors that make you feel sleepy. Sleep pressure.
- Caffeine has a similar shape to adenosine. It sits in the receptors and
  blocks them, without activating them. The tiredness is still there; the
  signal is not getting through.
- A useful image: caffeine is not fuel, it is someone putting tape over the
  fuel gauge.

Act III: Why it stops working.
- Tolerance: with regular use, the brain grows more adenosine receptors,
  so you need more caffeine to block the same share.
- Withdrawal headaches: skip your coffee, and the extra receptors are all
  available at once. [VERIFY mechanism details]
- Half-life of about five hours in most adults, varying widely from about
  2 to 10. Pregnancy, the contraceptive pill and smoking all change it.
  [VERIFY]
- Genetics: variation in the liver enzyme CYP1A2 makes some people fast
  and some slow metabolisers, which is why one person sleeps after an
  espresso and another lies awake.
- The afternoon coffee and sleep: studies showing caffeine six hours before
  bed still cuts sleep. [VERIFY the 2013 Drake study]

Act IV: The history of the bean.
- The Kaldi legend: an Ethiopian goatherd whose goats danced after eating
  berries. Present it as a legend, first recorded centuries later.
- Sufi monasteries in Yemen in the fifteenth century used coffee to stay
  awake for night prayers. The earliest solid evidence. [VERIFY]
- The India connection: Baba Budan, a Sufi saint, is said to have smuggled
  seven coffee beans from Mocha, strapped to his chest, around the
  seventeenth century, and planted them in the Chandragiri hills of
  Karnataka (the Baba Budan Giri range, near Chikmagalur). Hence Indian
  coffee. Treat it as tradition. [VERIFY]
- Coffeehouses in Istanbul, London and Vienna as places of news, argument
  and revolution. Bans on coffee in Mecca (1511) and by rulers who feared
  what people said over it. [VERIFY]
- South Indian filter coffee, the "tumbler and dabarah", and the India
  Coffee House as an institution of left-wing argument. The blog's own
  territory.

Act V: The popular claims, checked.
- "Wait 90 minutes after waking for your first coffee because of cortisol":
  weak evidence. [VERIFY what exists]
- "Coffee dehydrates you": mostly not, in habitual drinkers.
- "Coffee is bad for your heart": large studies now suggest moderate coffee
  is neutral or modestly associated with lower mortality. These are
  observational, so do not overclaim.
- The coffee nap: drink it, then nap for 20 minutes before it kicks in.
  Small studies only.

Act VI (the companion teaser): the first drink.
- Alcohol does the opposite of caffeine in some ways: it boosts GABA, the
  brain's brake. Set up a companion piece, "The Second Coffee and the First
  Drink", or keep this one about coffee only.

Aspects to cover:
- Tea against coffee: the same molecule plus L-theanine. Why tea feels
  different. [VERIFY evidence]
- Decaf: how caffeine is removed (Swiss Water Process: a Swiss link).
  [VERIFY history]
- Energy drinks and caffeine limits: about 400 mg a day for most adults per
  regulators. [VERIFY]
- Children, pregnancy, anxiety.
- Why coffee tastes different at altitude (loosely), and why it is bitter.

Possible asides:
- "The professor would like it on record that he read every study in this
  essay after his second cup, and they did nothing."
- On the insecticide: plants did not invent caffeine for us. We are simply
  the only species that asks for a refill.

Closing image:
The third cup going cold on the desk. What you needed was not more coffee;
it was the thing coffee was hiding. The honest cure for tiredness is still
the oldest one.

Plate idea: a single tumbler and dabarah of South Indian filter coffee,
steam rising, on a desk covered in notes. Or seven coffee beans on a Sufi's
open palm.

Length: 3 to 4 pours.

Claims to verify:
- Runge and Goethe.
- The caffeine-as-pesticide and bee study.
- Adenosine mechanism details.
- Half-life ranges.
- CYP1A2.
- The Drake 2013 study.
- The Kaldi legend's first recording.
- Yemen Sufi evidence.
- The Baba Budan story and dates.
- The Mecca ban of 1511.
- The cortisol claim.
- Mortality studies.
- L-theanine.
- The Swiss Water Process history.
- The 400 mg guidance.

Starting sources: Michael Pollan, "Caffeine" (audiobook essay); Matthew
Walker, "Why We Sleep" (use with caution; some claims are disputed); Mark
Pendergrast, "Uncommon Grounds"; EFSA caffeine opinion (2015).

5. THE HOLES IN THE CHEESE
------------------------------------------------------------------------------

Dek: For a century, everyone "knew" why Swiss cheese has holes. Then the
holes started disappearing, and Swiss scientists had to find out why.
The answer was hay.

The question: Why does Emmental have holes, and why did they start to
vanish?

Opening scene:
A slice of Emmental held up to the light, like a child does, looking
through the holes. The cheesemakers call them "eyes". Then the puzzle:
around the early 2000s, Swiss cheesemakers noticed fewer and fewer eyes.
Cheese without holes is called "blind". [VERIFY the "blind" term]

Story arc:

Act I: The textbook answer.
- 1917: the American scientist William Clark publishes the explanation.
  Bacteria (Propionibacterium freudenreichii) eat the lactic acid in the
  cheese and release carbon dioxide, which forms bubbles as the cheese
  ripens in warm cellars. [VERIFY]
- This is true, and it is the reason the holes grow. But it did not
  explain where the bubbles started, or why they were disappearing.

Act II: The mystery.
- Over the previous 15 years or so, fewer holes. Same bacteria, same
  recipes. What changed?
- The answer: the milking.

Act III: The experiment.
- 2015: researchers at Agroscope, the Swiss federal agricultural research
  institute, with the Swiss Federal Laboratories for Materials Science and
  Technology (Empa), added tiny amounts of hay dust to milk and made
  cheese.
- More hay dust meant more holes. Microscopic hay particles act as nuclei
  where the gas gathers, like dust in a cloud making raindrops.
  [VERIFY dosing and details]
- They used CT scanning, the same technique used on patients, to count the
  eyes inside whole wheels without cutting them. [VERIFY]
- Why the holes vanished: modern milking is closed and clean, with milk
  going straight from cow to sealed tank, so no hay dust falls into the
  bucket as it did in the open barn.

Act IV: What this tells us about science.
- A textbook answer can be right and incomplete at the same time.
- Progress (hygiene) had an invisible side effect (no nuclei).
- Nucleation is everywhere:
  - bubbles in champagne, which form on dust and fibres in the glass;
  - raindrops;
  - Mentos in cola;
  - boiling water in a scratched pan.
  One idea explains all of them.

Act V: The Swiss cheese model.
- A twist: in 1990 the British psychologist James Reason proposed the
  "Swiss cheese model" of accidents. Every safety layer has holes, and
  disasters happen when the holes line up. It is used in aviation,
  hospitals, and famously in COVID messaging (Ian Mackay's version).
  [VERIFY]
- The joke: the holes the model relies on are, it turns out, a result of
  dirty milking.

Aspects to cover:
- Emmental against Gruyère: Gruyère AOP should have few or no holes. Not
  all Swiss cheese is "Swiss cheese". [VERIFY AOP rule]
- "Swiss cheese" in America means something else entirely.
- Cheese, cows and Alpine transhumance, briefly.
- The Swiss relationship to precision and to cheese: the Cheese Union
  cartel, 1914–1999. [VERIFY; it is a great story]

Possible asides:
- "The professor notes that the cleaner the Swiss became, the fewer holes
  their cheese had. There is a lesson about the Swiss in here, and he is
  too polite to draw it."
- On textbooks: the most dangerous sentence in science is "everyone
  knows".

Closing image:
The slice held up to the light again. Each hole began as a speck of hay
from a summer meadow. The cheese is, in a small way, a record of the barn.

Plate idea: a wheel of Emmental cut open, with a single blade of hay lying
beside it. Or a CT-scan style image of a cheese wheel, holes glowing.

Length: 1 to 2 pours. A light palate-cleanser after a long essay.

Claims to verify:
- Clark 1917.
- The bacterium name.
- Agroscope/Empa 2015 details and the use of CT scanning.
- The "blind" term.
- The Gruyère AOP hole rule.
- The James Reason date.
- The Swiss Cheese Union dates.

Starting sources: Agroscope press release (May 2015); news reports in The
Guardian and Smithsonian; James Reason, "Human Error" (1990).

6. THE SUPERBUG NAMED AFTER DELHI
------------------------------------------------------------------------------

Dek: In 2010, a scientific paper named a new antibiotic-resistance gene
after India's capital, and the Indian government was furious. Behind the
diplomatic row was a much bigger story: the drugs that made modern medicine
possible are failing, and the reasons run through hospitals, farms, rivers
and pharmacies in every country, including India.

The question: How do bacteria beat antibiotics, and why is it one of the
biggest threats in medicine?

Opening scene:
A Swedish patient of Indian origin, treated in hospitals in New Delhi,
returns to Sweden in 2008 with an infection that almost nothing can treat.
Doctors find a gene they have never seen. It is named "New Delhi
metallo-beta-lactamase-1": NDM-1. [VERIFY case details]

Story arc:

Act I: The miracle.
- 1928: Alexander Fleming notices mould killing bacteria on a petri dish.
  Penicillin.
- It is not used widely until Florey and Chain's work in the 1940s and the
  wartime production effort.
- Before antibiotics:
  - a scratch could kill;
  - childbirth was dangerous;
  - surgery was a gamble.
- 1945: in his Nobel lecture, Fleming warns that misuse could make bacteria

7. THE NUMBER THAT TRAVELLED
------------------------------------------------------------------------------

Dek: The number you learned before you could read, zero, and the ten
digits you write every day, took more than a thousand years to travel from
India to Europe. They went through Baghdad, a merchant's son in Pisa, and a
ban in Florence. And the oldest written zero is still the subject of a
scientific quarrel.

The question: Where did zero and our numbers come from, and why did it
take the world so long to accept them?

Opening scene:
A small temple carved into the rock of Gwalior Fort, the Chaturbhuj temple.
An inscription dated to 876 CE records a garden 270 hastas long, and there
it is: a small round 0, one of the oldest undisputed zeros written as a
digit. Tourists walk past it. [VERIFY details]

Story arc:

Act I: Counting without zero.
- The Romans: try multiplying MCMXCIV by XLVII. Arithmetic was done on the
  abacus and only the answers were written down.
- Babylonian place-value numbers had a placeholder but no zero as a
  number. The Maya invented a zero independently. Credit both.
- The difference between zero as a placeholder (an empty column) and zero
  as a number you can add, subtract and multiply.

Act II: India's contribution.
- Place-value decimal notation with nine digits and a zero, developed in
  India in the early centuries CE.
- Śūnya: the Sanskrit word for empty or void. The link to philosophy
  (Nagarjuna's śūnyatā) is often claimed; present it as a suggestive
  parallel, not proven cause.
- 628 CE: Brahmagupta's Brāhmasphuṭasiddhānta gives rules for arithmetic
  with zero and negative numbers ("fortunes" and "debts"). He also gets
  one thing wrong: he says zero divided by zero is zero. Division by zero
  is still a trap. [VERIFY]
- Quote a line in Sanskrit (Devanagari plus IAST), as the blog does,
  if a verse is available. [VERIFY verse text]

Act III: The Bakhshali quarrel.
- A birch-bark manuscript dug up in a field near Peshawar in 1881, now at
  the Bodleian Library in Oxford.
- 2017: carbon dating suggested some folios were from the 3rd or 4th
  century CE, which would make its dots the oldest recorded zeros.
  Headlines everywhere.
- Then critics pointed out that different folios dated centuries apart,
  which is odd for one manuscript, and argued the dating was flawed or
  misread.
- Present the debate honestly: a science story about how to handle a claim
  you want to be true. [VERIFY all details]

Act IV: Through Baghdad.
- The House of Wisdom in Abbasid Baghdad. Indian astronomical texts arrive
  and are translated (the Sindhind).
- Around 825, al-Khwarizmi writes a book on calculating with Indian
  numerals. The Arabic original is lost, and a Latin translation begins
  "Dixit Algorizmi": so said al-Khwarizmi. His name becomes "algorithm".
  His other book gives "algebra".
- Arabs call the numerals Indian. Europeans later call them Arabic. Both
  are sort of right.
- śūnya becomes Arabic ṣifr (empty), then Latin zephirum, then Italian
  zefiro, then "zero". And ṣifr also becomes "cipher". [VERIFY chain]

Act V: Resistance in Europe.
- 1202: Leonardo of Pisa, Fibonacci, the son of a merchant who learned the
  numerals in North Africa (Bugia, in Algeria), writes Liber Abaci and
  sells them to Europe's merchants. The rabbit problem and the Fibonacci
  sequence (which Indian prosody had described centuries earlier, in
  Pingala and Hemachandra). [VERIFY]
- 1299: Florence's bankers' guild bans the new numerals in account books,
  because a 0 can easily be turned into a 6 or a 9, and forgery was a real
  worry. [VERIFY]
- Centuries of the abacists against the algorists. The new numbers won
  with printing and double-entry bookkeeping.

Act VI: Why zero matters.
- Without zero:
  - no decimal arithmetic for everyone;
  - no algebra as we know it;
  - no calculus;
  - no binary.
- Every computer runs on 1s and 0s: the last stop of the number that
  travelled.
- A reflection on credit: why "Arabic numerals" is the name, and why a
  more accurate name would be "Hindu–Arabic".

Aspects to cover:
- The difficulty of dating ideas as against dating objects.
- Nationalist overclaiming (every discovery claimed for ancient India) and
  Eurocentric underclaiming (Indian mathematics ignored). The essay should
  avoid both.
- The Kerala school (Madhava, Nilakantha) and infinite series before
  Newton and Leibniz: a teaser for a future piece. [VERIFY]
- Zero in philosophy and religion.

Possible asides:
- The professor strictly disagrees with WhatsApp claims that ancient India
  invented everything, and equally with textbooks that act as if Europe
  invented arithmetic. Both are bad history.
- "Brahmagupta divided zero by zero and got zero. Fourteen hundred years
  later, spreadsheets still show #DIV/0!. Some problems are just hard."

Closing image:
The Gwalior inscription again, the small circle in the rock, and on the
reader's phone screen a billion zeros a second. The oldest idea in the
room is running the newest machine.

Plate idea: the Gwalior "270" inscription, with the zero highlighted. Or a
birch-bark leaf with a single dot, in soft light.

Length: 5 pours.

- The etymology chain.

"The Universal History of Numbers" (use critically); the Bodleian 2017

8. TWO MOUNTAIN RANGES, MELTING
------------------------------------------------------------------------------

Dek: In Switzerland, people wrap glaciers in white blankets in summer and
hold funerals for the ones that die. In the Himalaya, glaciers feed rivers
that nearly two billion people depend on. The ice is the most honest
thermometer on earth. Here is what it is saying, in the two places I call
home.

The question: How fast are glaciers melting, how do we know, and what does
it mean for the Alps and the Himalaya?

Opening scene:
September 2019, the Pizol glacier in eastern Switzerland: a few hundred
people in black walk up a mountain for a funeral, for a glacier that had
shrunk too small to count as one. [VERIFY] Then cut to the Rhône Glacier,
where white fleece blankets are spread over the ice every summer to slow
the melt around the ice grotto for tourists. [VERIFY]

Story arc:

Act I: What a glacier is.
- Snow that does not melt, compressed over years into ice, flowing
  downhill under its own weight like a very slow river.
- Mass balance: a glacier is a bank account. Snow in winter is deposit;
  melt in summer is withdrawal. For decades, withdrawals have been larger.
- How glaciologists measure it:
  - stakes drilled into the ice and read every year;
  - satellites measuring height and gravity (GRACE);
  - old photographs and paintings compared with today.
  Switzerland has some of the longest records in the world. [VERIFY]

Act II: The Alps.
- GLAMOS, the Swiss glacier monitoring network: Swiss glaciers lost about
  6 per cent of their volume in 2022 and about 4 per cent in 2023,
  roughly 10 per cent in two years. [VERIFY exact figures]
- Why those years: little winter snow, then long hot summers. The Saharan
  dust that darkened the snow so it absorbed more heat.
- Uncovered by the melt:
  - old aircraft wreckage;
  - bodies of climbers missing for decades;
  - a 1926 Swiss–Italian border line that had to be redrawn because it
    followed a ridge of ice that disappeared. [VERIFY]
- Projections: most Alpine glaciers could be gone by 2100 under high
  emissions. [VERIFY with GLAMOS/ETH figures]

Act III: The Himalaya.
- The "Third Pole": the largest store of ice outside the polar regions.
- ICIMOD's 2023 report: Hindu Kush Himalaya glaciers melted 65 per cent
  faster in the 2010s than the previous decade, and could lose up to 80
  per cent of their volume by 2100 under high emissions. [VERIFY]
- Rivers:
  - the Ganga, Indus and Brahmaputra;
  - meltwater's share differs hugely, being high in the Indus and lower in
    the Ganga, where the monsoon dominates. Do not overclaim. [VERIFY]
- "Peak water": melt first increases river flow, then declines once the
  ice is gone. The good years now may be borrowed from the future.
- Glacial lake outburst floods:
  - the 2013 Kedarnath disaster;
  - the 2021 Chamoli flood;
  - Sikkim's South Lhonak lake bursting in October 2023, destroying the
    Teesta III dam.
  [VERIFY each; be careful about causes, since Chamoli was a rock-ice
  avalanche]

Act IV: The IPCC's embarrassment.
- The 2007 IPCC report claimed Himalayan glaciers could vanish by 2035. It
  was wrong, based on a misquoted source, and the IPCC apologised in 2010.
  [VERIFY]
- Why this matters: a single error was used to attack the whole field.
  The real science is alarming enough without exaggeration. It is an
  honest lesson for the essay, and for the blog.

Act V: What people are doing.
- Switzerland:
  - the June 2023 referendum approving a climate law aiming at net zero by
    2050, passed with about 59 per cent;
  - the KlimaSeniorinnen case at the European Court of Human Rights
    (April 2024), which found Switzerland had violated older women's rights
    by not doing enough on climate.
  [VERIFY both]
- Ladakh: Sonam Wangchuk's "ice stupas", artificial glaciers built in
  winter to store water for spring planting. An engineer's answer with
  limits. [VERIFY]
- Glacier blankets, and why they cannot scale.
- The only real solution is emissions. Adaptation buys time; it does not
  refreeze anything.

Act VI: Ice as an archive.
- Ice cores from glaciers hold centuries of air, dust and pollution. Lead
  from Roman smelting is found in Greenland ice. [VERIFY]
- The Ice Memory project: scientists taking cores from melting glaciers
  and storing them in Antarctica, before the record disappears.
- When a glacier melts, it does not just lose water. It loses history.

Aspects to cover:
- Albedo: why white snow reflects heat and dark ice absorbs it, a feedback
  loop.
- Black carbon from cooking stoves and diesel darkening Himalayan snow.
- Water politics: the Indus Waters Treaty (1960) and its suspension by
  India in April 2025. [VERIFY, and be careful and neutral]
- Tourism, skiing and the economics of snow.
- Glacier names and memory: the Pizol funeral, Iceland's Okjökull plaque

Possible asides:
- On the IPCC 2035 error: "The professor notes that the fastest way to
  help people who deny the evidence is to exaggerate it."
- On the blankets: "Switzerland is tucking its glaciers in at night. It is
  very Swiss, and it is very sad."

Closing image:
Two places, one view. From a Swiss train window, a valley where the
glacier on the old postcard is now bare rock. And a village in Ladakh
building an ice stupa by hand in January. Both are trying to hold on to
winter.

photograph and today.

PART 1: THE RULES, A SHORT HISTORY OF MODERN TRADE
------------------------------------------------------------------------

PART 2: THE MONEY
------------------------------------------------------------------------

Act VI: How a cross-border payment actually works.
- There is no single global bank. Banks hold accounts with each other
  (correspondent banking, nostro and vostro accounts).
- SWIFT (founded 1973, based near Brussels) does not move money. It
  carries the messages that tell banks to move it. [VERIFY]
- Dollar payments finally settle through US banks (CHIPS and Fedwire).
  This is why the US can sanction almost anyone: the dollar runs through
  New York.
- Letters of credit: a bank promises to pay the seller when shipping
  documents are presented. A medieval invention (Italian merchants, and
  India's own hundi) still used for a large share of trade. [VERIFY]
- The bill of lading: the document that is the cargo, legally.
- Incoterms (FOB, CIF and so on), set by the International Chamber of
  Commerce in Paris since 1936: who pays for what and when risk passes.
  [VERIFY]
- Hawala and hundi: trust-based transfer systems, older than banks; legal
  and illegal uses.
- Remittances: India is the world's largest recipient, well over $100
  billion a year. [VERIFY latest figure]

Act VII: The dollar's empire.
- The figures:
  - roughly 58 per cent of global central bank reserves are dollars;
  - the dollar is on one side of almost 90 per cent of foreign exchange
    trades;
  - much of trade invoicing is in dollars even between non-US countries.
  [VERIFY IMF COFER and BIS figures]

25. GENEVA WRITES THE RULES
------------------------------------------------------------------------------

Dek: The size of a shipping container, the A4 sheet in your printer, the
+91 before an Indian phone number, the letters CHF and INR on every
currency exchange, the rules of world trade, and the orbital slot of the
satellite that tells your phone where it is. All of them were agreed, and
most are still maintained, within a few kilometres of the lake in Geneva.

The question: Who makes the rules that let the world trade, talk and pay,
and why so many of them sit in one small Swiss city?

Opening scene:
A walk along the Place des Nations and up the hill:
- the ITU tower;
- WIPO's glass building;
- the WTO in the lakeside Centre William Rappard, built in 1926 for the
  International Labour Organization, with murals of workers still on its
  walls;
- ISO's offices a tram ride away.
Nobody looks twice. [VERIFY building history]

Story arc:

Act I: The telegraph needed a treaty.
- 17 May 1865, Paris: twenty states found the International Telegraph
  Union so that messages could cross borders: common codes, tariffs and
  rules. It is now the oldest UN agency, and it moved to Geneva after the
  Second World War. [VERIFY founding members and move date]
- The ITU today:
  - radio spectrum;
  - satellite orbital slots;
  - country calling codes (+41, +91);
  - video standards;
  - the World Radiocommunication Conferences, where countries fight over
    frequencies.

Act II: The post.
- 1874, the Treaty of Bern founds the Universal Postal Union. Any letter
  can go anywhere, and the sending country keeps the stamp money. Still
  headquartered in Bern. (It sets up idea 29.)

Act III: Standards.
- ISO, founded in 1947 and based in Geneva. What a standard is, and why
  agreeing on boring things makes everything else possible.
- ISO 668: container sizes (link to "The Box").
- ISO 216: A4 paper, based on a German standard of 1922 and on the
  beautiful ratio of 1 to the square root of 2, so a sheet folded in half
  keeps its shape. [VERIFY]
- ISO 3166 and ISO 4217: country and currency codes (CH, IN, CHF, INR).
- ISO 20022: the language of modern payment messages, which SWIFT and
  national systems moved to. (Link to 12 and 24.) [VERIFY SWIFT migration
  dates and UPI's use]
- The IEC (1906, Geneva): electrical standards, and why plugs still differ
  anyway.
- UNECE's WP.29: global vehicle rules, from seatbelts to headlights.
  [VERIFY]

Act IV: Ideas as property.
- The Paris Convention (1883) and the Berne Convention (1886): the first
  international treaties on patents and copyright. WIPO (1967), in Geneva.
- India and patents: the 1970 Patents Act, which allowed Indian generic
  medicines (link to the pharmacy of the world). The TRIPS agreement at
  the WTO (1995) and the 2001 Doha declaration on public health.
  [VERIFY]

Act V: Trade.
- GATT (1947) and the WTO (1995), both in Geneva. How WTO disputes work,
  and why the system has been stuck since 2019.
- India at the WTO: food security, fisheries subsidies, and the
  e-commerce moratorium. [VERIFY current issues]

Act VI: Why Geneva.
- The League of Nations (1920); Swiss neutrality; Red Cross history;
  the international city.
- The cost: Geneva's international sector and its economic weight.
  [VERIFY figures]
- The criticism: technocratic, slow, dominated by powerful countries.
  The defence: the alternative is no rules.

Aspects to cover:

- The Greenwich meridian was decided in Washington, not Geneva (link to
  the time-zone idea). Not everything is Swiss.
- The BIPM in Sèvres, France, which keeps the metre and coordinates world

26. THE FLAGS OF NOBODY
------------------------------------------------------------------------------

Dek: A ship owned by a Greek family, managed from Singapore, chartered by
a Swiss trader, insured in London and crewed by Filipinos and Indians flies

- Abandonment: owners who vanish, leaving crews unpaid in foreign ports

Act III: The pirates.

  [VERIFY peak figures]

Act IV: The shadow fleet.

- Environmental risk: old ships, uncertain insurance, a spill waiting to

27. THE COFFEE HOUSE THAT INSURES THE WORLD
------------------------------------------------------------------------------

Dek: Nothing moves without insurance, and modern insurance began over
coffee. In a London coffee house in the 1680s, merchants met to share the
risk of ships lost at sea. The system that grew from it now decides
whether ships sail through war zones, and climate change is testing it
first.

The question: How does the world price risk, and what happens when risk
becomes too big to insure?

Opening scene:
Edward Lloyd's coffee house on Tower Street, London, around 1688:
merchants, captains and gossip about ships. Men write their names under a
description of a ship and its voyage, each taking a share of the risk:
hence "underwriters". [VERIFY date and etymology] Then cut to the
modern Lloyd's building, pipes on the outside, where the Lutine Bell was
traditionally rung for news of a ship lost. [VERIFY]

Story arc:

Act I: Sharing the loss.
- Ancient ideas: Babylonian merchants, Chinese traders spreading cargo
  across boats, and the Rhodian sea law of "general average": if cargo is
  thrown overboard to save a ship, everyone shares the loss. [VERIFY]
- General average still exists: the Ever Given's owners declared it, so
  cargo owners shared the costs. [VERIFY]
- India's own history of risk-sharing among merchants, and hundi (link to
  idea 24). [VERIFY]

Act II: Lloyd's.
- From coffee house to market. Lloyd's List (1734), one of the oldest
  newspapers.
- How the Lloyd's market works: syndicates, and, historically, the
  "Names" with unlimited liability, many of whom were ruined in the 1990s
  asbestos losses. [VERIFY]
- Lloyd's past in the slave trade, and its 2020 apology. Handle it
  honestly. [VERIFY]

Act III: Insuring the insurers.
- 1861: a fire destroys much of the Swiss town of Glarus, and local
  insurers cannot cover it.
- 1863: Swiss Re is founded in Zurich to spread risk further.
  Reinsurance: insurance for insurance companies. [VERIFY]
- Munich Re, Swiss Re, and now Bermuda as a reinsurance centre.
- Why reinsurers are among the most serious students of climate data. Swiss
  Re's sigma reports.

Act IV: War risk.
- Marine war-risk insurance: premiums jump when a zone is declared
  dangerous.
- The Red Sea since late 2023: premiums rose to a percentage of the ship's
  value per voyage, making the long route round Africa cheaper despite
  the fuel. Insurance, as much as missiles, rerouted world trade.
  [VERIFY figures]
- P&I clubs (protection and indemnity), mutual associations that cover
  most of the world's ocean-going fleet, and their role in the Russian oil
  price cap. [VERIFY]

Act V: Climate is breaking insurance.
- Insured losses from natural catastrophes have climbed above $100 billion
  in many recent years. [VERIFY]
- Insurers pulling out of parts of California and Florida. Homes that
  cannot be insured cannot get mortgages.
- The "protection gap": most losses in poorer countries are uninsured.
  [VERIFY figures]
- India's low insurance penetration, and its crop insurance scheme
  (PMFBY). [VERIFY]

Act VI: New tools.
- Parametric insurance, which pays automatically when a trigger is hit
  (rainfall below X, wind above Y), with no loss adjuster needed. Used for
  farmers, and for the Caribbean's catastrophe pool. [VERIFY]
- Catastrophe bonds: investors bet against hurricanes.
- The honest conclusion: insurance can spread risk, but it cannot make a
  risk go away. Past a point, prices tell you where not to build.

Aspects to cover:
- Moral hazard.
- Actuaries and the history of probability (Halley's life tables).
  [VERIFY]
- Life insurance in India: LIC as an institution.
- The Titanic and other famous claims. Be careful with facts.

Possible asides:
- "The professor notes that the whole global system of risk began with
  men drinking coffee and gossiping about ships, which is also a fair
  description of this blog."
- On climate: when insurers, who are not famous for sentiment, start
  leaving a region, it is not politics. It is arithmetic.

A coffee cup on a table, and a slip of paper with names written under a

28. THE CLOCK IN THE SKY
------------------------------------------------------------------------------

Dek: Everyone knows GPS tells you where you are. Fewer know that its real
product is time: atomic time broadcast from space, which phone networks,
power grids, stock exchanges and card payments quietly synchronise to. And
in parts of the world today, someone is lying to it.

The question: How does satellite navigation work, why does so much of
modern life depend on its clocks, and what happens when they are jammed
or faked?

Opening scene:
2017, the Black Sea: the captains of around twenty ships see their GPS
position suddenly jump to an inland airport, 30 km away. They have not
moved. Their navigation is being spoofed. [VERIFY] Then 2024–25: pilots

37. THE GROUND BENEATH EVERYTHING, PART TWO: RARE EARTHS AND THE CRITICAL

The question: What are critical minerals, why does China dominate them,

38. THE GRID
------------------------------------------------------------------------------

Dek: Everything in this blog assumes the lights are on. They are on
because of the largest machine humanity has ever built: the electricity
grid, spinning at exactly 50 cycles a second across a whole continent. It
was first knitted together across borders at a substation on the Swiss
border in 1958, and in 2003 a tree touching a Swiss power line helped
black out all of Italy.

The question: How does electricity actually get to the socket, why do
grids fail, and why are they now the bottleneck for clean energy?

Opening scene:
28 September 2003, around 3 a.m.: a high-voltage line in the Swiss Alps
sags in the heat and touches a tree. A chain of failures follows, and
minutes later almost all of Italy, some 56 million people, is dark. It is
Rome's "White Night" festival; people are stranded in the metro. [VERIFY
details] Then the other end of scale: 30 and 31 July 2012, India, where
the largest blackout in history leaves around 600 million people without
power. [VERIFY]

Story arc:

Act I: The war of the currents.
- Edison's direct current against Tesla and Westinghouse's alternating
  current. AC wins because transformers let it travel far at high
  voltage.
- Why 50 Hz in Europe and India but 60 Hz in the Americas, and why Japan
  uses both (a legacy of German and American generators in the 1890s).
  [VERIFY]

Act II: The machine that must balance every second.
- Supply must equal demand every moment. The frequency is the pulse: if
  demand exceeds supply, it drops, and if it drops too far, generators
  trip to protect themselves.
- Inertia: the spinning mass of big generators that steadies the grid, and
  why solar and wind (with no spinning mass) change the physics. [VERIFY
  explanation]

Act III: The Star of Laufenburg.
- 1958: the grids of Switzerland, Germany and France are linked at
  Laufenburg on the Rhine, the "Star of Laufenburg", the birth of the
  interconnected European grid. [VERIFY]
- Today the synchronous European grid runs from Portugal to Turkey, and
  Ukraine joined it in an emergency in March 2022, cutting its link to
  Russia's grid. [VERIFY]
- Switzerland: one of Europe's busiest electricity crossroads, with many
  cross-border lines and pumped hydro used as Europe's battery (link to
  "The Battery in Your Pocket"). The unresolved question of a Swiss–EU
  electricity agreement. [VERIFY current status]

Act IV: India's one grid.
- From five regional grids to "One Nation, One Grid" (synchronised in
  2013), after the 2012 blackout. [VERIFY]
- The distribution problem: state utilities' losses, power theft, and free
  electricity for farmers pumping groundwater (link to the monsoon and
  food ideas). [VERIFY figures]
- Trade with neighbours: hydropower imports from Bhutan, Nepal selling
  power to India and, through India, to Bangladesh. [VERIFY]
- The "One Sun, One World, One Grid" vision (2018) of linking solar across
  time zones. [VERIFY]

Act V: When it breaks.
- The Iberian blackout of 28 April 2025. [VERIFY cause findings]
- The 2021 Texas freeze.
- The 2003 Italy blackout.
- Cyberattacks: Ukraine's grid, 2015. [VERIFY]
- Black start: how you restart a grid from zero.

Act VI: The bottleneck.
- Solar and wind are cheap, but connecting them is slow. Long queues of
  projects waiting for grid connections in many countries. [VERIFY]
- High-voltage direct current (HVDC) lines crossing continents and seas.
- A shortage of transformers, with years-long waiting times. [VERIFY]
- Storage, demand response and smart meters.

Aspects to cover:

  electrified, and what "electrified" means. [VERIFY]

41. THE ARMS TRADE
------------------------------------------------------------------------------

Dek: Weapons are traded like anything else: through contracts, credit,
shipping and diplomacy. India has for years been the world's largest arms
importer and is now trying to become an exporter. Switzerland, the
famously neutral country, makes and sells weapons, and in 2022 refused to
let Germany send Swiss-made ammunition to Ukraine. Geneva hosts the talks
that are meant to limit all of it.

The question: How does the global arms trade work, why is India so
dependent on it, and what does neutrality mean for a country that sells
weapons?

Tone note: even-handed and factual, with no glorification and no
moralising. The data should do the work.

Opening scene:
2022: Germany asks Switzerland to allow re-export of Swiss-made
anti-aircraft ammunition for the Gepard tanks it is sending to Ukraine.
Switzerland says no, citing neutrality and its own export law. [VERIFY]
Then, half a world away, a BrahMos missile battery being handed over to
the Philippines, India's first major missile export. [VERIFY]

Story arc:

Act I: How the arms trade works.
- Government-to-government deals, private companies, offsets (where the
  seller must invest in the buyer's country), and end-user certificates
  (promises about who will use the weapons).
- SIPRI in Stockholm measures it; the top exporters are the US, France and
  Russia; the top importers include India, Saudi Arabia, Qatar, Ukraine
  and Pakistan. [VERIFY latest SIPRI period]

Act II: India, the great importer.
- Why:
  - long borders;
  - two hostile neighbours;
  - a Cold War reliance on Soviet arms;
  - a slow domestic industry.
- The shift from Russia towards France, the US and Israel. The Rafale
  deal. [VERIFY]
- The Bofors scandal (1986–87): a Swedish gun, alleged kickbacks, and a
  government that fell partly over it. [VERIFY framing]
- "Atmanirbhar Bharat" in defence: import bans on certain items, the Tejas
  fighter, and private companies entering. [VERIFY]
- Exports: BrahMos to the Philippines, artillery and ammunition to
  Armenia, and rising export figures. [VERIFY]

Act III: Swiss neutrality and Swiss weapons.
- Neutrality under the 1907 Hague Conventions: a neutral state must not
  favour warring parties in arms exports from its own government. [VERIFY]
- The War Material Act: Swiss arms cannot be re-exported to countries at
  war without Swiss permission. The 2022–23 refusals to Germany, Denmark
  and Spain, and the backlash from European partners. [VERIFY]
- Swiss arms industry: RUAG, Pilatus trainer aircraft, and ammunition
  makers. Past controversies (Pilatus aircraft used in conflicts, the
  Oerlikon history). [VERIFY]
- The 2020 initiative to ban investment in arms makers, rejected. [VERIFY]
- The debate over whether neutrality still means what it did.

Act IV: Geneva and the effort to limit it all.
- The Conference on Disarmament in Geneva: the treaties it produced (the
  Chemical Weapons Convention negotiations and the Comprehensive Nuclear
  Test Ban Treaty) and its deadlock since 1996. [VERIFY]
- The Arms Trade Treaty (2014), whose secretariat is in Geneva. India and
  others have not joined it, with India's stated reasons. [VERIFY]

42. WHEN THE CHAIN BROKE
------------------------------------------------------------------------------

Dek: For two years, the whole machinery of global trade was put through a
stress test nobody designed:
- masks ran out;
- cars sat unfinished for want of a chip;
- a single ship blocked the Suez Canal;

The question: What did the pandemic reveal about how the world makes and
moves things, and what changed afterwards?

43. FLYING
------------------------------------------------------------------------------

Dek: Every passenger plane is also a cargo plane: underneath the seats,
its belly carries medicines, fresh fish, phones and post. Air travel is
governed by a treaty written in Chicago in 1944 that lists "freedoms of
the air". Its trade body keeps a big office in Geneva, India's national
airline has come full circle back to the family that founded it, and
Switzerland's airline died in one day in 2001 and came back.

The question: How does flying work as a global system, and what does it
carry besides us?

Opening scene:
2 October 2001, Zurich: Swissair runs out of money to pay for fuel, and

plane carrying mail from Karachi to Bombay, the first flight of what

Story arc:

Act I: The freedoms of the air.

  Montreal). The "freedoms": the right to overfly, to land, to carry

Act III: The Gulf hubs.

44. THE MEDICINE CHAIN
------------------------------------------------------------------------------

Dek: A pill of generic medicine bought in New York may have been made in
Gujarat, from an active ingredient made in China, and approved after a
patent fight in which a Swiss company lost to India in the Supreme Court.
India is the "pharmacy of the world" for cheap medicines. Switzerland,
around Basel, is one of the capitals of expensive ones. Between them runs
the most important supply chain for human life.

The question: How are medicines made and moved around the world, why are
the cheapest ones Indian and the newest ones Swiss or American, and how
fragile is the chain?

Opening scene:
2001: Yusuf Hamied, the head of the Indian drugmaker Cipla, offers to
supply a three-drug HIV treatment for under a dollar a day, at a time
when branded versions cost many thousands of dollars a year. It changes
AIDS treatment in Africa. [VERIFY figures] Then 1 April 2013: India's
Supreme Court rules against Novartis, the Basel giant, refusing a patent
on a new form of its cancer drug Glivec. [VERIFY]

Story arc:

Act I: How a medicine is made.
- The active pharmaceutical ingredient (API): the chemical that does the
  work. Key starting materials, intermediates, then formulation into
  tablets, capsules and injections.
- The global split: research and brands in the US, Switzerland and
  Europe; APIs increasingly in China; generic formulation in India.
  [VERIFY]
- Biologics and vaccines: grown in living cells, harder to copy
  (biosimilars).

Act II: India, the pharmacy of the world.
- The 1970 Patents Act: India allowed patents on processes, not products,
  so Indian firms could make medicines by new routes. The birth of the
  generics industry. [VERIFY]
- India supplies a large share of generic medicines to the US by volume
  and much of the world's vaccines, through Serum Institute. [VERIFY
  figures]
- The dependence problem: India imports a large share of its APIs and key
  starting materials from China. Production-linked incentives since 2020
  to change it. [VERIFY shares]

Act III: Switzerland, the pharmacy of the rich.
- Basel: from dyes to drugs, Ciba, Geigy, Sandoz (now Novartis) and
  Roche. Pharma as a very large share of Swiss exports. [VERIFY]
- Research, patents, and very high prices for new drugs.
- The 2025 US tariff threats on pharmaceuticals and Swiss imports, and
  the negotiations. [VERIFY carefully]
- Lonza in Visp (link to 42).

Act IV: The patent fights.
- TRIPS at the WTO (1995), and the requirement that India grant product
  patents from 2005. [VERIFY] (Link to "Geneva Writes the Rules".)

  extend a patent). The Novartis Glivec case. [VERIFY]
- India's first compulsory licence, Natco and Bayer's cancer drug Nexavar
  (2012). [VERIFY]
- The balance: incentives for innovation against access to medicine.
  Present both sides seriously.

Act V: When the chain fails.
- Quality:
  - the 2022 deaths of around 70 children in The Gambia linked to cough

  [VERIFY]
- The 2008 heparin contamination from China. [VERIFY]
- Shortages: cheap generic injectables and antibiotics with too few
  makers; cancer drug shortages in the US and Europe. [VERIFY]
- The cold chain for vaccines and insulin (link to "The Scientist Nobody
  Funded").

Act VI: Price and access.
- Why the same medicine costs wildly different amounts in different
  countries.
- India's Jan Aushadhi generic stores and price controls. [VERIFY]
- Insulin: a century-old discovery, its patent sold for a dollar, still
  unaffordable for many. [VERIFY]
