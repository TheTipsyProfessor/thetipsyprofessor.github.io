On Losing Arguments in Four Scripts

Being wrong in your own language is embarrassing. Being wrong in someone else’s is an education, and cheaper than most.

I have lost arguments in four scripts now and I can report that the humiliation is identical in each, which is either a fact about language or a fact about me.

What differs is the shape of the loss. In English I lose slowly, by concession, the way a coastline goes. In the others I lose all at once, because I run out of vocabulary at exactly the moment the argument gets interesting, and whatever I say next is a smaller, stupider version of what I meant.There is a specific flavour of defeat in hearing yourself say the B1 version of a thought you had in full. You can watch the other person receive the simpler argument and, generously, address it.

Japanese

その晩、港は港のふりをやめた。 I learned this sentence from a novel and deployed it, unwisely, in a conversation about urban planning.

The man I was talking to — an architect, patient to a fault — pointed out that I had said something beautiful and irrelevant, and that Japanese gives you an enormous number of ways to be beautiful and irrelevant, and that this is exactly why the language rewards people who shut up.He said this much more politely. The politeness is doing a lot of work in the original and none at all in my translation, which is its own small lesson.

Every language has a default failure mode for foreigners. In Japanese, mine is sounding lovely and meaning nothing.

Hindi

सबसे अच्छी बातचीत आख़िरी बस छूटने के बाद होती है। — the best conversations happen after the last bus has gone. I did not come up with this. It was said to me, flatly, as an explanation for why nobody was leaving.

I argued that this was survivorship bias: of course the conversations you remember are the late ones, because the early ones end and you go home and forget them. I was pleased with this. I had the whole thing lined up.

Then somebody asked me, in Hindi, whether I had ever once stayed late and regretted it, and I could not answer in Hindi, and by the time I had the English ready the moment had gone and everyone had agreed I was wrong. Which I was.

Note the mechanism. The argument was not refuted. It was simply out-waited by a room with better stamina than mine.

Arabic

أفضل الأحاديث تبدأ بعد أن تفوت آخر حافلة.

The same sentiment, offered to me a year later in Amman by somebody who had never met the man in Delhi, in a language that reads the other way. I said so. I said it was remarkable that two cultures had arrived at the same proverb independently.

I was told, kindly, that it is not a proverb, that he had just made it up, and that I had a bad habit of promoting things people say into things people believe.This is the single most useful criticism I have received about the way I write, and I received it from a man waiting for a taxi. Losing that one cost me a paragraph I had already drafted in my head on the way over.

Greek

Τὸ δὶς ἐξαμαρτεῖν οὐκ ἀνδρὸς σοφοῦ. To err twice is not the mark of a wise man.

I quoted this at somebody. In the original. Unprompted. At a dinner.

There is no recovering from that and I am not going to attempt it here.

The one thing I have actually learned

If you diagram it, the failure is always the same. Let c be what I can construct in the language and t what I actually think. The argument I make is not t; it is the projection of t onto the space I can reach:

\text{what I say} \;=\; \operatorname{proj}_{C}(t), \qquad C \subsetneq T

and the gap \lVert t - \operatorname{proj}_{C}(t)\rVert is not noise. It is the whole difference between a position and a slogan.

The uncomfortable follow-on, which took me four scripts and about nine years to arrive at: this is also what happens in English. C is just bigger, so I mistake it for T. Every argument anyone makes is the projection of what they think onto what they can say, and the fluent are simply better at not noticing the difference.

The value of being wrong abroad is that you can see the seam. At home the seam is still there. You have just stopped being able to find it.

I have not become a better arguer. I have become somebody who assumes the other person meant more than they managed, which is slower, and worse for winning, and the only genuinely useful thing I have taken from any of this.

On “pours”

One pour is about six minutes — roughly how long a drink lasts if you are paying attention to it. A minute count would be a claim about how fast you read, made by someone who has never met you.
