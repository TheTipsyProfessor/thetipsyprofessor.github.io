The Harbour Was a Rumour

Three nights in Reykjavík, awake for most of them, learning that a place will not introduce itself until you have stopped expecting it to.

On the third night the harbour stopped pretending to be a harbour and became a rumour.I want to be precise about this, because it sounds like the sort of thing people write about the north and do not mean. The fog came in at roughly one in the morning and by half past there were no boats, no water, no far side — only the sound of a hull knocking against a tyre, somewhere, at the speed of something very patient. I had been awake for nineteen hours and the coffee had gone cold twice.

This is the part of travelling that nobody sells you, and it is the only part I have ever been able to write about. Not the arrival. Not the photograph from the headland with the light doing what the light does here. The long, stupid middle — the fourth hour of a bus ride, the closed museum, the two in the morning when the place finally runs out of things to perform and just sits there being itself.

He will tell you he walks to think. He walks because he cannot sit still in a room he is paying for.

I walked out past the old slipway where the tourist boats are tied up for the winter, their names in a language I can read the shape of and not the sense of. Sæfari. Andrea. Hafrún.A Norwegian in the hostel kitchen told me, with total confidence, that Hafrún means “sea secret.” A woman from Akureyri later told me, with more confidence and better grammar, that he had made that up. I have chosen to keep his version in the part of my head that is not responsible for facts. Someone had left a thermos on a bollard. It was still warm. I have thought about that thermos more than I have thought about the northern lights.

What the guidebook gets wrong

The guidebook allocates Reykjavík a day and a half. This is not wrong in the way that a factual error is wrong. It is wrong in the way that describing a conversation by its duration is wrong.

Boredom is not the absence of the place. Boredom is the place, finally, with nothing left to show you.

I have started keeping a different kind of note. Not what I saw — what I was still doing after I had run out of things to see. In Lisbon that was watching a man fail to parallel-park for eleven minutes. In Tbilisi it was a power cut and four strangers’ phone torches. Here it was the fog, and the knocking, and the thermos.

The video above is not of Reykjavík and is not, strictly, of anything. I put it here because I wanted you to have the option of some duration. Most travel writing hands you the highlight and keeps the four hours to itself. That seems to me like handing someone the punchline.

The arithmetic of a bad night

There is a rule I have been refining for years, and this trip is where it finally held. Call h the hours you are awake past the point of usefulness, and s the number of things you actually see in them. Then:

\lim_{h \to \infty} \frac{s}{h} = 0 \qquad\text{and yet}\qquad \frac{\partial\,(\text{what you remember})}{\partial h} > 0

Which is to say: the longer you stay up, the less you see per hour, and the more of it stays.I am aware this is not a theorem. It is a grievance with notation. I do not have an explanation. I have a thermos.

The flight home left at six. I did not sleep on it either. Somewhere over the Atlantic the man beside me asked how I had found Iceland and I said foggy, which was true, and useless, and the only honest thing I had.

You will know a place has let you in when you stop being able to summarise it.

I have been trying to summarise Reykjavík for three weeks. This is as close as I have got, and it is mostly about a thermos.

On “pours”

One pour is about six minutes — roughly how long a drink lasts if you are paying attention to it. A minute count would be a claim about how fast you read, made by someone who has never met you.
