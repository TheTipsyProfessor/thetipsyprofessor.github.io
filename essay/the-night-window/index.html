The Night Window

A twenty-four-hour place in Osaka, three people who did not know each other, and the best conversation I have ever failed to follow.

The window at the back of a twenty-four-hour place in Nishinari opens onto a car park and a vending machine, and at four in the morning it is the most sociable address in the city.

I was there because I am always there, somewhere, at four in the morning.See seventeen things I do instead of sleeping, item seventeen, the one that works. There were three others: a man in his sixties in a suit jacket over a tracksuit, a woman with a bicycle she never stopped holding, and a younger man who said almost nothing and laughed at everything.

What I could follow

Maybe one word in nine. しんどい — I had that one. まだ — that one too. The rest went past at the speed of people who have known each other for six weeks or six years, and I could not have told you which.

What I could follow perfectly was the shape. The suit jacket made an assertion. The bicycle disagreed, but held the disagreement for a beat before delivering it, which meant she liked him. The younger man laughed early, before the joke landed, which meant he was not following either and had decided to be pleasant about it. I understood everything except the content.

You can be entirely locked out of what people are saying and still be let all the way into what is happening.

The moment worth the four in the morning

At some point the suit jacket said something short and the other two went quiet.

Not awkward — the other quiet, the one where a thing has been said correctly. The woman looked at her handlebars. The younger man stopped laughing, and did not start again, and that was the most information I got all night.

He has tried four times to find out what was said. The phrase he reconstructed phonetically turns out, according to two separate and equally embarrassed Japanese speakers, to be nothing at all.

Then the bicycle left, and the younger man left, and the suit jacket and I stood not talking for another ten minutes in a way that was completely fine, and then he tipped his head at me about a centimetre and went too.

Why I keep writing these down

Because the alternative account of that night is I could not speak the language, so nothing happened — and that is false, and it is the story a lot of people tell themselves about a lot of countries.

Something happened. Four people arranged themselves around a window at the worst hour of the day, and a thing was said that landed, and everybody left in the right order. I have been at dinner parties in my own language where less occurred.

Not understanding is a poor excuse for not paying attention. Usually it’s the opposite: it’s the only time you’re paying attention to anything but the words.

I went back the following night. Nobody was there. That is also how this works, and it is why I do not recommend it to anyone as a plan.

On “pours”

One pour is about six minutes — roughly how long a drink lasts if you are paying attention to it. A minute count would be a claim about how fast you read, made by someone who has never met you.
