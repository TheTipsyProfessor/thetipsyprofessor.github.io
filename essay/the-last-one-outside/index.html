The Last One Outside

A short defence of standing in the cold with strangers, written by someone who does not smoke and keeps going out anyway.

I do not smoke. I go outside with the smokers in every city I have ever drunk in, and I have never once been asked to explain myself, which tells you something about the arrangement.

The smoking area is the only place left in public life where talking to a stranger requires no pretext at all. Not a queue — a queue has an end. Not a bar — a bar has a barman, a menu, a reason to turn away. Four minutes under an awning with nothing to do and no way to leave early without seeming odd.The four minutes are load-bearing. Fewer and nobody starts; more and it becomes a conversation, with all the obligations that implies. A cigarette is almost exactly the right length of excuse.

Every good social technology has a built-in ending. That’s what makes people willing to begin.

What I have collected out there

A structural engineer in Tbilisi explaining, with a finger on a wet railing, why the building opposite was going to be a problem in about nine years.

A woman in Tallinn who had been to the same terrible conference I had been to and hated a different talk, which turned into the most useful forty minutes of the trip and happened entirely in the cold.

Note what these have in common: neither person would have spoken to him indoors, and he would not have spoken to them. The awning did all of it.

Two men in Porto having an argument about a football transfer so technical and so bitter that when they noticed me listening they simply widened the circle by a step and carried on, which is the most generous thing anybody has done for me in a year.

The part I am less pleased about

It works because of a thing I would vote to abolish.

I am not confused about this. The cigarettes are killing them, the four minutes exist because of the cigarettes, and I am standing there enjoying the sociology while a man coughs. There is no version of this essay where that is fine, and I have stopped trying to write one.

What I want is the shape without the cause: somewhere outside, briefly, no service, no seating, no reason to be there other than being there, with a natural end. Nobody has built it. Nobody will, because the whole point is that you are not there for the thing, and you cannot sell people a place they are not there for.

The smoking area is the last piece of common ground in the building, and it is made of a bad idea.

Until then I will keep going out at midnight in a coat I did not bring, standing next to people slowly harming themselves, learning things. It is not a defensible position. It is just where the conversation is.

On “pours”

One pour is about six minutes — roughly how long a drink lasts if you are paying attention to it. A minute count would be a claim about how fast you read, made by someone who has never met you.
