The Papaya Rows

Four hours in a plantation in Kerala, doing nothing useful, with a man who had no interest in explaining anything to me.

The rows run east to west so the fruit ripens on one side first. I learned this four hours in, by accident, from a man who had spent the entire afternoon not telling me things.

His name was Baiju and he was not a guide. I want to be exact about that, because the whole value of the afternoon depended on it. He was a man walking his own rows on a Thursday, and I had attached myself to him in the way tourists do, and he had permitted it in the way people do when refusing would take more words than allowing.He asked me exactly two questions in four hours. The first was whether I had eaten. The second, much later, was whether I always walked this slowly, which I have decided was a joke.

What a guided version would have been

Ninety minutes. A laminated card about കാരിക്ക. Three facts about yield per hectare. A photograph of me holding a papaya, taken by him, framed so the rows go back to the horizon.

I know this because I have taken the guided version of this afternoon in four countries and I could not tell you one thing from any of them.

Being shown a place and being let into it are different transactions, and only one of them takes all afternoon.

What actually happened

We walked. He checked things. I did not know what he was checking and he did not offer. At some point he cut a fruit that looked identical to every other fruit, tasted it, and threw it into the undergrowth with an expression of mild personal disappointment, and I understood that I had just watched an entire professional judgement happen and had no access to any of it.

This is the closest the professor gets to a religious experience: watching somebody be extremely good at something he cannot evaluate.

Around three the heat stopped being weather and became a fact about the body. We sat. He smoked. I did not, which I regretted, only because it left me with nothing to do with my hands while doing nothing.

And then — no transition, no preamble — he said the thing about the rows running east to west, and pointed, and I saw that one flank of every tree was heavier than the other, and that I had been walking through an obvious fact for four hours without seeing it.

The exchange rate

There is an arithmetic to this that I have never been able to make come out nicely. Call k the number of facts acquired and h the hours spent. The guided afternoon has an excellent ratio:

\frac{k_{\text{guided}}}{h_{\text{guided}}} \;=\; \frac{14}{1.5} \;\approx\; 9 \qquad \frac{k_{\text{Baiju}}}{h_{\text{Baiju}}} \;=\; \frac{1}{4} \;=\; 0.25

Thirty-six times worse. And I remember the one.

Nobody has ever been taught anything at the rate the schedule promised.

I bought four papayas I had no way to carry and no way to refrigerate, and gave three of them to the family at the guesthouse, and ate the fourth over a sink at eleven at night like a man committing a small crime. East side first. It was better. I have no idea whether that is true or whether I simply wanted it to be, and after some thought I have decided that this is the correct note to end on.

On “pours”

One pour is about six minutes — roughly how long a drink lasts if you are paying attention to it. A minute count would be a claim about how fast you read, made by someone who has never met you.
