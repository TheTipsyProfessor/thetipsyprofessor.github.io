Against Cairns

In praise of leaving a place exactly as unmarked as you found it, argued badly by a man who has built several.

There is a particular kind of person who arrives at a lake nobody has ruined yet and immediately begins improving it. I have been that person. I have the photographs.

A cairn is a real thing with a real job: on high ground in bad weather it is the difference between a route and a guess.The Norwegian word is varde. The ones that matter are large, old, maintained by people who know the ground, and placed where a person in fog would actually look for them. Nothing I have ever built qualifies on any of those counts. What I built by that lake was not that. What I built was a signature.

The argument against, which is mostly correct

Stacking stones moves the stones. Under the stones are things that live under stones. On a scree slope you are dismantling the only stable ground a lot of small life has.Park services in Utah, Iceland and Scotland have all spent real money and real hours taking these apart. That is the part that should have embarrassed me out of it years earlier than it did: somebody is paid to undo the thing I did for free, for fun.

He built one anyway, two days after writing the first draft of this. He would like that on the record before someone else puts it there.

And a false cairn in poor visibility is not a neutral object. It is a sentence in a language hikers are obliged to trust, saying this way, written by someone who did not know the way and was not thinking about anyone arriving later.

A mark you leave for no one but yourself is not a mark. It is litter with better lighting.

Where my own argument gets uncomfortable

I walked past nineteen of them on the way up.

Nineteen. I counted, because counting is what I do instead of thinking. And somewhere around the eleventh I noticed that I had stopped seeing the lake and started reading the shore — that the stacks had turned a place into a comments section, and I was scrolling it.

That is the real objection and it has nothing to do with lichen. The stones were fine. I was the one who had been changed. A landscape with nineteen little monuments in it is a landscape that has been pre-interpreted, and the interpretation is always the same, and the interpretation is somebody was here and wanted you to know.

The rule I have settled on

Not “leave no trace” — that is a slogan, and I distrust anything I can put on a water bottle.

The rule is narrower and I can actually keep it: build nothing another person could mistake for information. A cairn is information. A scratched name is information. A bent branch at a junction is information. All of those enter a system other people are relying on, and adding noise to a system you are not maintaining is the whole of the thing I object to.

The mountain does not need your annotation. It is not a library book.

Everything else — sitting a long time, taking the photograph, being moved — costs nobody anything, and is what you came for. The stones were never the point. I knew that going up. I rebuilt one anyway on the way down, out of what I can only describe as spite toward my own essay, and then I took it apart again, which is possibly worse.

On “pours”
